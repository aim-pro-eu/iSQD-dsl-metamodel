# iSQD (Intelligent-SQD) — Framework Synthesis

> A practical synthesis of the iSQD (Intelligent-SQD) pedagogical framework: what each synthesis, category, and subcategory is, **when to use it** (context, learner profiles), and **how it looks in the real world**.
>
> Sources (the synthesis is based solely on these documents):
>
> - Zhou, Tondeur & Cromphaut (in press). *Transferability of SQD Strategies to Teacher AI Competency Development: A Systematic Review* (`SQD2_iSQD.pdf`) — the meta-aggregation that produced the Intelligent-SQD model, including the SQD2 baseline it builds on (Tondeur et al., 2025).
> - AIM-PRO D5.1 (Tondeur, Howard, Cromphaut & Zhu, 2026). *Training strategies for the development of AI competencies* (`isqd_requirements.pdf`) — the canonical iSQD strategy statements, activity list (S1–S6 / C1–C12 / SC1–SC32), implementation guidelines, and learner personas.
> - iSQD codebook (`ISQD_codebook.pdf`) — operational definitions of every subcategory with verbatim data excerpts from the 29 underlying studies (the source of all "in practice" examples; "No.x" references point to these studies).
> - AIM-PRO monthly meeting presentation, 2 April 2026 — *AIM-PRO Pedagogical Constitution* (WP5, T5.1/T5.2): how iSQD is institutionalized as the set of pedagogical principles guiding AIM-PRO's tools, resources, and learning scenarios.

## 1. What is iSQD?

iSQD is an evidence-based framework that answers one question: **which training strategies actually work for developing AI competencies in real practice?** It tells program designers, trainers, and institutional leaders *what to do, in what sequence, and for whom* when preparing people to use AI competently and responsibly.

### Lineage

| Model | Basis | What it contributed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SQD** (Synthesis of Qualitative Data, 2012) | First synthesis of qualitative evidence on preparing teachers for technology | Six micro-level strategies: role models, reflection, design, collaboration, scaffolded authentic experiences, continuous feedback |
| **SQD2** (Tondeur et al., 2025) | Replicated systematic review of 12 years of new qualitative evidence | Six syntheses across three levels (micro: individual training; meso: institutional conditions; macro: ongoing technological change and theory–practice alignment); added Digital Identity, Instructional Design Models, and the Affective Dimension |
| **iSQD / Intelligent-SQD** (Zhou et al., in press) | Meta-aggregation of **29 qualitative studies** (PRISMA; Web of Science 2015–2025; 640 records screened; K-12 or pre-service teachers; 21 journals, 14 countries; JBI quality appraisal) | Retains SQD2's six dimensions — confirming their robustness — but re-derives the specific strategies for the AI context: **32 subcategories in 12 categories under 6 syntheses** |

The AIM-PRO document extends iSQD's application beyond teachers to any professional developing AI competencies (its personas include an HR director and a CS student), while the empirical base remains teacher training.

### Architecture at a glance

| Synthesis | Strategy statement | Categories | Subcategories |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **S1 Familiarization** 🟢🟡 | "Progressively build AI knowledge and experiences, supported with targeted scenarios for using AI and structured guidance towards open exploration are essential to familiarise individuals with AI technologies." | C1 AI onboarding · C2 AI-supported scenarios · C3 AI progressive exploration | SC1–SC6 |
| **S2 Reflective practice** 🟢🟡🔵 | "Individuals must critically reflect on the potential, the limitations and place of AI for both their practice and their profession." | C4 Reflective practice on AI | SC7–SC11 |
| **S3 Collaborative design** 🟢🟡🔵 | "To design AI-rich materials, collaborative activities need to support interactions between humans and help them share resources and strategies, while utilizing the iterative interactions to design with the technology." | C5 Collaboration · C6 AI-supported design | SC12–SC17 |
| **S4 A cycle of authentic experiences** 🟢🟡🔵 | "Practicing and refining AI-integration and adapting AI-content to their authentic setting, feedback is needed to support individuals to use AI in their practice." | C7 AI-integrated teaching cycle · C8 AI-integrated teaching improvement | SC18–SC22 |
| **S5 Affective dimension** 🟡 | "Emotional reactions, attitudes and beliefs, and self-efficacy should be attended to when preparing individuals for developing their AI competencies." | C9 Affective dimension | SC23–SC25 |
| **S6 Institutional vision** 🟢🟡 | *(derived)* "AI competence development requires institutional conditions — curriculum, frameworks, training and incentives, guidelines, and leadership support — so that support is systemic rather than left to individual effort." | C10 AI-integrated curriculum · C11 AI-focused training & incentives · C12 Institutional support | SC26–SC32 |

**Levels:** S1–S5 are *micro-level* strategies applied to the individual learner; S6 is the *meso level* — the institutional conditions that make S1–S5 work and last. All of this sits inside the *macro level*: ongoing technological change and the alignment of theory and practice (a design principle, not an actionable strategy).

### Three kinds of strategies

The systematic review classifies the iSQD strategies relative to SQD2 (markers used throughout this document):

- 🟢 **Transferred** — explicitly named as working in the AI context exactly as in SQD2 (e.g., *Modelling AI practices*, *Open exploration*, *Documented reflection*, *Reflective discussion*, *Peer support*, *microteaching*, *AI-integrated classroom teaching*, *AI-related professional learning*).
- 🟡 **Adapted / reconfigured** — the review's explicitly named adaptations (e.g., the three-phase Familiarization, *Co-creation with AI*, *AI-informed pedagogical adaptation*, the reconfigured Affective Dimension, *Discipline-specific AI integration*, *Institutional AI guidelines*), plus subcategories reorganized into SQD2 categories that the review does not classify individually.
- 🔵 **New** — no counterpart in SQD2; responds to what generative AI uniquely demands: **Output evaluation, Role negotiation, Iterative prompting, Emotional enrichment of AI content, Responsible AI use**.

Key interpretive findings from the review that shape *when* to use these strategies:

- The **intermediary phase of AI-supported scenarios** exists because AI's conceptual complexity requires an explicit step between initial knowledge acquisition and independent exploration.
- **Co-creation with AI** signals a shift from designing *with* technology as an instrument to designing *through dialogue* with AI as a generative partner.
- **Responsible AI use** works best **embedded in practice activities**, not as a standalone ethics topic — ethical AI competency develops through situated judgment.
- **Institutional AI guidelines** respond to the normative uncertainty that characterizes AI use in education; **discipline-specific integration** responds to the fact that AI affordances vary substantially across subject areas.
- **Trust in AI-generated outputs** emerged as a prominent concern across the affective, reflective, and institutional dimensions.

## 2. How to apply the framework

### Implementation principles

Five guidelines from the AIM-PRO document govern how the syntheses are selected and sequenced:

1. **The strategies are interrelated.** "Designing" goes hand-in-hand with "authentic experiences"; the categories are so intertwined they work best as a coordinated set. Treat the framework as a system, not a menu — design programs where S1 feeds S3, S3 feeds S4, S2 and S5 run through all of them, and S6 sustains it all.
2. **Adapt to individual profiles ("one size does not fit all").** Learners arrive with unique combinations of attitudes, self-efficacy, and prior experience:
   - **High-attitude learners** (e.g., Marco) thrive through **collaboration and peer-based (re)design** of materials.
   - **Low-attitude or low-self-efficacy learners** (e.g., Elena) need an emphasis on **continuous feedback and confirmation** to mitigate insecurity and anxiety.
   - The **Affective Dimension** (S5) is the lever for overcoming psychological barriers for *all* backgrounds.
3. **Sensitivity to equity.** Move beyond "access to devices" to deeper tech gaps: disparities in digital competence among learners, intercultural awareness (practices must not marginalize diverse cultural backgrounds), and support for learners with learning difficulties so technology acts as an enabler, never a barrier.
4. **Moving toward adaptive, smart learning.** The end-state is an environment that uses data and AI to give personalized, automated support — e.g., a struggling learner is prompted in real time with specific feedback or a suggestion to collaborate.
5. **Program-wide, holistic integration.** AI training must not be a stand-alone event; it should be woven systematically through the whole curriculum, institution, or company, so learners encounter the strategies in multiple contexts (online, hybrid, blended).

### Decision guide — who to start with what

| Learner profile | Lead with | Then layer in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newcomer or sceptical professional (low AI self-efficacy; e.g., Elena) | **S1** — knowledge, modelling, guided scenarios, small steps to avoid overload | S5 (anxiety work) · S2 (guided reflection on outputs) · S4 (low-stakes pilot) |
| Confident, high-attitude tech user (e.g., Marco) | **S3** — collaborative design, peer design critique | S2 (output evaluation, role negotiation) · S4 (authentic project work) · S5 (digital identity/values) |
| Experienced practitioner, moderate AI confidence (e.g., Dr. Clara) | **S2** — structured reflection on AI's role, limits, ethics | S3 (framework-based co-design, TPACK) · S4 (iterative classroom cycles) · S6 (institutional guidelines) |
| Anyone, at any point | **S5** is continuous — check emotions, beliefs, self-efficacy in every phase | — |
| Program/department/company designer | **S6** first (curriculum, frameworks, incentives, guidelines, leadership), then sequence S1 → S4 | S5 embedded throughout |

## 3. Synthesis 1 — Familiarization 🟢🟡

> **Strategy statement:** "Progressively build AI knowledge and experiences, supported with targeted scenarios for using AI and structured guidance towards open exploration are essential to familiarise individuals with AI technologies."

**Description.** Familiarization is the entry stage where individuals first encounter AI. SQD2's two-phase structure (modelling → exploration) is **reconceptualized into three phases** in iSQD: (1) *AI onboarding* — systematic knowledge building and modelling; (2) *AI-supported scenarios* — experiencing tool affordances and case-based instruction; (3) *progressive exploration* — from scaffolded to open. AI is explicitly an "emerging technology," so watching a demonstration is not enough: learners need hands-on experience to build confidence and understand practical utility before professional application. The extra middle phase exists because AI's **conceptual complexity** requires an intermediary step between knowing and doing.

**When to use.**

- *Context:* the first phase of any AI-competency program — teacher education, university courses, corporate upskilling, or self-directed learning — before learners touch real tasks with real learners/customers.
- *Learners:* anyone with limited AI experience, and especially **sceptical, anxious, or low-self-efficacy professionals** (e.g., Elena), who need controlled exposure to prevent cognitive overload; bite-sized, scaffolded formats directly reduce AI anxiety (14 of 19 teachers in one study reported this). Advanced learners (Marco) can compress this phase.

**In practice.** A teacher-education module opens with an overview of AI, defining key concepts (AI, GenAI, LLMs) (No.10). A five-week practicum teaches the fundamental principles of using AI in language instruction, including practical applications of ChatGPT, Pi, Sider, and Copilot for lesson planning and tutoring (No.12). For Elena, the equivalent entry point is *HR-specific* guided AI scenarios — AI-assisted screening and reporting — in a controlled context, while observing role models (other SME managers) demonstrate tangible AI benefits.

### C1 — AI onboarding

**Description.** The foundational first step: systematic explanation of AI concepts, principles, application potential, and usage strategies, combined with observing experts' or peers' AI practice in action.

**When to use.** At the very beginning of training, before any hands-on work; with cohorts that have little or no AI background; with sceptics who need conceptual grounding and visible proof that "someone like me" can do this; a prerequisite for exploration activities.

**In practice.** Workshop 1 pairs a macro-level introduction to ChatGPT, its educational applications, and prompt-giving principles with the fundamentals of writing feedback, so AI knowledge is anchored to a professional skill (No.29). A five-week practicum covers fundamental principles plus practical applications with ChatGPT, Pi, Sider, and Copilot (No.12).

#### SC1 — Building AI knowledge 🟡

- **Description.** Systematic explanation of AI concepts, principles, application potential, and usage strategies by teacher educators.
- **When to use.** First sessions of any program; learners with no prior AI exposure; sceptical learners who need to understand *what AI can and cannot do* before trying it.
- **In practice.** A language-teacher module opening with definitions of AI, GenAI, and LLMs, then a systematic tour of how these map to teaching tasks (No.10); a workshop pairing a macro introduction to ChatGPT with the fundamentals of writing feedback (No.29); a practicum in which participants are taught the fundamental principles of using AI in language instruction, including using ChatGPT, Pi, Sider, and Copilot for lesson planning and tutoring (No.12).

#### SC2 — Modelling AI practices 🟢

- **Description.** Indirect AI usage experiences through modelling by experts, teacher educators, or peers using AI (direct transfer of SQD2's *role models*).
- **When to use.** Before or alongside first hands-on attempts; with **low-self-efficacy learners** and novices — vicarious observation builds confidence before hands-on risk; also for sceptical audiences who trust seeing over being told (Elena: fellow SME managers presenting concrete AI wins in HR processes).
- **In practice.** Teacher educators who "demonstrate to students how to use AI… model some ideas of how to use AI to generate ideas or generate feedback" because students "might need to be explicitly shown how to use it properly" (No.20). A five-minute hands-on demonstration video showing three key steps — initial assessment of the student's essay, interacting with ChatGPT, strategic revisions — which participants can repeatedly re-watch during group practice; one student teacher: *"Without the classroom demonstrations, I would have been struggling for a long time… it boosted my confidence"* (PST01/PST04, No.29).

### C2 — AI-supported scenarios 🟡

**Description.** The added middle phase that bridges knowledge and exploration: learners experience what AI tools *can do* in contexts like their own, by mapping tools to functions to situations and by analysing real cases of AI-integrated practice. It responds to AI's conceptual complexity.

**When to use.** After basic onboarding and before open exploration; when learners need to see **relevance to their own professional context** (their subject, their job role); when building tool-selection literacy ("which tool for which task") rather than mastery of one tool; for in-service teachers who must see AI applied *in context* before designing anything themselves.

**In practice.** A teacher's weekly reflections show a mature tool–function–context map: *ChatGPT* for differentiated task creation, *Perplexity* for cultural content, *Magic School* for visual grammar explanations, *Twee/Dift.me* for practice activities — "I now understand each tool's strengths and combine them to meet my diverse student needs" (No.14). A lecturer displays an example on screen ("the grading scale in Austria ranges from 1 to ___") to show how adding contextual information changes the specificity and accuracy of AI responses (No.4).

#### SC3 — Experiencing affordances of AI tools 🟡

- **Description.** Presentation of AI tools and their functions to establish tool–function–context mappings.
- **When to use.** Mid-familiarization, once basic concepts exist; when learners must choose among many tools and the goal is informed *tool selection*; especially useful for practitioners juggling multiple platforms (schools, enterprises) with concrete instructional needs (differentiation, assessment, content creation).
- **In practice.** A module in which trainees examine various AI tools for creating educational content: Eduaide and Magic School for lesson/unit plans and teaching resources, Differ for differentiated instruction, Quizziz for assessments, and Microsoft Coaches as an example of intelligent tutoring and adaptive assessment (No.10). A microlearning course in which a teacher sets specific goals per tool: "Today, I used Alayna.us for lesson planning, Perplexity for researching authentic materials, and Magic School for creating listening comprehension tasks" (No.14). Teachers combining tools when one falls short: "If ChatGPT's writing feedback were not detailed enough, I would try Magic School's approach" (No.14).

#### SC4 — AI case-based instruction 🟡

- **Description.** Review and analysis of AI-integrated instructional cases demonstrating AI use in context, including well-structured "model cases" (problem and solution presented) that prepare learners for ill-structured case discussions.
- **When to use.** To bridge theory and practice; when learners need to *see AI used in a realistic situation* before doing it themselves; in problem-based and case-based teaching designs; immediately before learners must produce their own AI-integrated solutions.
- **In practice.** Between a lecture and a case-based discussion in which teachers must develop instructional solutions, the program introduces additional model cases in which problems *and* solutions are presented, with well-structured problems (e.g., rule-using problems) so teachers can see how new theories and tools are applied in contexts before tackling ill-structured problems (No.8). The Austria grading-scale example is used explicitly to "prepare them for understanding the role of context" in shaping AI responses (No.4).

### C3 — AI progressive exploration

**Description.** The movement from structured, guided task completion toward open, interest-driven experimentation. Scaffolding is gradually released as confidence and skill grow.

**When to use.** After knowledge and scenarios are in place. **Anxious or low-confidence learners** should spend longer in scaffolded exploration (SC5); **high-attitude, confident learners** (Marco) can move quickly to open exploration (SC6) — but should not skip scaffolding entirely if prompting skills are weak.

**In practice.** A microlearning sequence where teachers progress week by week — Week 2: "Breaking down GenAI learning into small chunks makes it less daunting. Today, I focused on designing communicative activities, learning to prompt ChatGPT, and practicing group tasks with Twee" (Teacher 7, No.14) — followed by free experimentation with ChatGPT, Perplexity, Elicit, and Connected Papers for academic writing (No.24).

#### SC5 — Scaffolded exploration 🟡

- **Description.** Guided AI task completion under structured support (worksheets, checklists, explicit task instructions, model prompts).
- **When to use.** Early hands-on practice; with **anxious or low-self-efficacy learners** (reduces cognitive load and AI anxiety — 14 of 19 teachers in one study said the bite-sized format helped them overcome AI anxiety); learners with limited prompting experience who need model prompts and explicit steps; in short, time-pressed workshops where outcomes must be reliable.
- **In practice.** Participants are given a checklist summarizing the session's principles and strategies as a scaffold to help them internalize knowledge during practice (No.29). Participants are provided with model prompts and allowed to fine-tune their interactions until they achieve desired outcomes — e.g., crafting prompts based on the specific task (creating an activity about a grammar point), age, proficiency level, and topic (No.19). Bite-sized weekly chunks: "This targeted approach helped me see how AI can enhance my lesson planning" (Teacher 7, No.14).

#### SC6 — Open exploration 🟢

- **Description.** Opportunities for open exploration of AI tools based on individual interests, encouraging experimentation and discovery (transfer of SQD2's *hands-on experience*).
- **When to use.** The final phase of familiarization; with **confident, self-directed learners** (e.g., Marco) who would find heavy scaffolding constraining; whenever motivation through autonomy and discovery is desired; at institutional level too — schools should allocate dedicated time for hands-on engagement with GenAI.
- **In practice.** Student teachers freely explore tools for their own interests: "I learned how a variety of AIs can be utilized in the spheres of academic and research writing. The use of ChatGPT, Perplexity, Elicit, and Connected Papers to search and consolidate relevant data was amazing" (No.24). Repeated hands-on practice builds confidence: "When I applied what I learned in practice, I became more familiar with how to interact with ChatGPT… I believe I can do better because we have had many opportunities to practice" (PST01/PST06, No.29). A recommendation that schools allocate dedicated time for teachers to engage with GenAI hands-on so they "can see the practical value of the technology" (No.3).

## 4. Synthesis 2 — Reflective practice 🟢🟡🔵

> **Strategy statement:** "Individuals must critically reflect on the potential, the limitations and place of AI for both their practice and their profession."

**Description.** Learners critically reflect on AI's role — its potential, its limits, and where it sits in their professional identity. iSQD adds two strategies with **no counterpart in SQD2**: *Output evaluation* (AI now produces instructional content autonomously, so it must be critically assessed — uncritical acceptance risks spreading inaccurate information and bias) and *Role negotiation* (navigating the evolving boundary between professional judgment and AI's generative capacity). A further iSQD breakthrough: **AI itself becomes a scaffold for reflection** — learners use AI as a partner to analyze their own pedagogical choices.

**When to use.**

- *Context:* throughout the program, intensifying as learners gain autonomy; whenever learners begin accepting AI outputs uncritically; in programs focused on quality, ethics, and integrity of AI-generated materials; in settings with ethical stakes (bias, privacy, transparency, accountability, academic integrity).
- *Learners:* **everyone** — but it is the *lead* strategy for confident AI users who risk over-reliance (Marco: needs critical evaluation of outputs and control over AI-supported workflows) and for experienced practitioners with integrity concerns (Dr. Clara: structured reflection on AI's role in teaching and assessment) or responsibility concerns (Elena: fairness and accountability).

**In practice.** In a 13-week GenAI course, weekly reflective journals with guided prompts were "the medium through which pre-service teachers solidified their understanding of the role of AI tools in teaching" — moving them "beyond the excitement of using a new gadget" to critical analysis of its pedagogical value (No.22). An image-generation bias activity: educators prompt image AIs with "a person," "a woman," "a house," "a street," "a plate of food," adding cultural/geographical variants, then collaboratively analyze how AI interprets prompts and where cultural bias appears (No.7).

### C4 — Reflective practice on AI

**Description.** The five reflective moves available in the AI context — negotiating human–AI role boundaries, evaluating AI outputs, documenting reflection, discussing in groups, and conversing with AI systems themselves — forming one coherent reflective system.

**When to use.** After first hands-on experiences (reflection needs material to reflect on); continuously as a habit; whenever AI-generated content enters the learner's professional work; structured formats are especially valuable for learners without a mentor and for groups working on sensitive topics (ethics, identity).

**In practice.** Participants learn "to be curators of technology tools' outputs, not just consumers": one writes that auto-generated problems "lacked the specific context I wanted for my class, so I had to tweak the wording… Technology tools can give a great starting point, but the teacher needs to shape it into a meaningful task" (No.22). A "critical friend" group in which members "challenged each other's assumptions, and offered diverse perspectives that enriched our understanding of GenAI technologies" (No.25).

#### SC7 — Role negotiation 🔵

- **Description.** Reflection on teacher–AI role boundaries and professional identity in AI-enhanced teaching contexts.
- **When to use.** With learners whose **professional identity may be challenged by AI** (teachers, HR, legal, creative roles); when signs of over-reliance appear; a core element of "digital identity" work; for sceptics worried about replacement (Elena: making explicit where AI supports decision-making and where human responsibility remains central, via decision rationales and evaluation checklists).
- **In practice.** A teacher in AI-enhanced training: *"No matter how advanced the tech gets, it shouldn't overshadow the personal connections we've built in our classrooms… I'm all for integrating AI, but in a way that complements, not replaces, the personal interaction that makes teaching so rewarding"* (Leo, No.17). Pre-service teachers positioning themselves as critical reviewers: "After ChatGPT has assessed the writing sample, it must be reviewed by humans again, based on the Gaokao rubric criteria" (PST03, No.29).

#### SC8 — Output evaluation 🔵

- **Description.** Critical reflection on AI-generated instructional materials — checking accuracy, alignment with curricular goals, bias, and inaccuracy rather than copy-pasting.
- **When to use.** **Whenever AI generates content autonomously** (i.e., the entire GenAI era); a mandatory habit before any AI material reaches learners; for confident users who accept first outputs (Marco); to correct misconceptions about AI's reliability; in assessment-sensitive disciplines (Dr. Clara: evaluating AI-generated materials and negotiating academic-integrity concerns).
- **In practice.** Pre-service teachers are "instructed to use AI-generated outputs critically — for example, checking for accuracy and alignment with curricular goals — rather than copy-pasting content" (No.22). The bias-uncovering activity: generating images for prompts like "a person," "a woman," "a house" with and without cultural descriptors, then "collaboratively analyz[ing] how AI systems interpret human input… enabling educators to critique AI-generated speculations in light of their own lived experiences" (No.7). Pre-service teachers revising ChatGPT's vague "try to propose some novel ideas" into specific, actionable feedback such as "propose some novel ideas or personal reflections, such as how I would approach the situation" (No.29).

#### SC9 — Documented reflection 🟢

- **Description.** Documentation and reflection on AI use through structured artefacts (reflective journals, portfolios, concept maps, blog entries).
- **When to use.** In longitudinal programs where growth must be tracked and made visible; when reflection needs structure (guided prompts); for accountability and later self-analysis.
- **In practice.** A 13-week course in which participants keep weekly structured reflective journals with guided prompts covering: learning experiences with weekly modules; application of GenAI tools in ELT contexts; challenges encountered and solutions developed; self-regulated learning strategies; and plans for future implementation — submitted on a regular cycle so instructors could monitor growth iteratively (No.14). "My reflection log helps me identify the best tool combinations for different teaching objectives" (No.14).

#### SC10 — Reflective discussion 🟢

- **Description.** Group discussions that foster critical reflection through multiple perspectives.
- **When to use.** In collaborative learning settings; to surface diverse views and challenge individual assumptions; when ethical or identity questions benefit from multiple viewpoints; one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost strategies available.
- **In practice.** The "critical friend" structure: "We provided each other with constructive feedback, challenged each other's assumptions, and offered diverse perspectives that enriched our understanding of GenAI technologies" (No.25). Teacher educators "engaging their students in reflective discussions about the implications of these tools on their learning and future professional practices" (No.20). Educators engaging "in the discussion and mapping process… collaboratively shape their understanding of AI integration in education and build knowledge together" (No.7).

#### SC11 — AI-mediated reflection 🟡

- **Description.** Reflective conversation with AI systems for automated support and immediate feedback — AI as a reflective partner (reconfigured from SQD2's technology scaffolding).
- **When to use.** When no human mentor is available (novice teachers, remote contexts, off-hours); to scale reflective support; also *as a modelling strategy* — watching a learner converse with AI teaches prompting and critical dialogue at once.
- **In practice.** "Since experienced teachers, mentors, or supervisors were not always present, it was important to make some decisions individually" — novice EFL teachers used ChatGPT as a reflective partner and discovered, through the conversation, that they over-talked: "ChatGPT highlighted the role of students' inclusion and engagement" (No.2). Pre-service teachers "resorted to GenAI tools such as ChatGPT and POE for pedagogical advice, grammar check, and content creation… as experienced teachers and experts" (No.28). Reflection conversations left novice teachers "more motivated, creative, and satisfied… and emotionally felt better" (No.2).

## 5. Synthesis 3 — Collaborative design 🟢🟡🔵

> **Strategy statement:** "To design AI-rich materials, collaborative activities need to support interactions between humans and help them share resources and strategies, while utilizing the iterative interactions to design with the technology."

**Description.** Designing AI-rich materials is a collaborative process — **between humans and with AI** — so the technology aligns with learning outcomes rather than novelty. Peers share experiences, materials, prompting strategies, and design outcomes in sustained communities; learners co-create instructional content with AI and adapt it to goals, learner levels, cultural contexts, and curriculum standards; and they develop *iterative prompting* skills through multi-turn dialogue. iSQD marks a shift in what "design" means: **from designing *with* technology as an instrument to designing *through* dialogue with AI as a generative partner.** Pedagogical reasoning — the *why* and *how* of using a specific AI tool for a specific outcome — is what separates competence from tool play.

**When to use.**

- *Context:* any program with a design component — lesson development, content production, assessment design, product or process design in corporate settings; subject-specific work where content knowledge is needed to refine outputs.
- *Learners:* **high-attitude, confident learners thrive here** (research shows they benefit most from collaboration and peer-based redesign — Marco's lead strategy). It also works for less confident learners, whose prompting weakness is another learner's strength (peer scaffolding).

**In practice.** In a TPACK-grounded workshop, pre-service teachers worked in groups on GenAI-enhanced lesson planning, comparing and refining each other's prompts; a student with weaker prompting skills: *"I chose to collaborate with other classmates to form a group. I could depend on them to refine the prompts and improve the outputs"* (No.18).

### C5 — Collaboration

**Description.** Human-to-human interaction that builds AI competencies: peer support, sharing of resources and strategies, and sustained learning communities beyond the course.

**When to use.** In group-based programs and workplaces; when skill levels within a cohort vary (peers compensate for each other's gaps); to build the social infrastructure that sustains adoption after the course ends; in communities of practice and resource-constrained settings.

**In practice.** Group work provided "peer scaffolding in developing the technological skills needed for GenAI-enhanced lesson planning"; high-quality vicarious learning experiences through "group discussions and collaborative activities" shaped self-efficacy and skills within a short period (No.18, No.24, No.29).

#### SC12 — Peer support 🟢

- **Description.** Building AI competencies and skills through peer interactions within learning (direct transfer of SQD2's *group work*).
- **When to use.** Mixed-skill cohorts; short training programs (fast self-efficacy gains); whenever one person's prompting weakness is another person's strength; to balance operational proficiency with reflective thinking.
- **In practice.** "I sometimes struggled to get the exact results I wanted… I chose to collaborate with other classmates to form a group. I could depend on them to refine the prompts and improve the outputs" (No.18). Groups created based on specialization finding "a better pedagogical approach" (No.24). A recommendation to "emphasize vicarious learning through group discussions and collaborative activities… particularly impactful in shaping PSTs' self-efficacy and skills within a short period" (No.29).

#### SC13 — AI resource & strategy sharing 🟡

- **Description.** Sharing of AI experiences, instructional materials, prompting strategies, and design outcomes.
- **When to use.** In communities of practice; resource-constrained settings (shared prompts and templates reduce duplication); to accumulate reusable assets across a team, school, or company; to accelerate skill acquisition via others' artifacts.
- **In practice.** A teacher: "I often found online forums and social media groups where other teachers shared tips and strategies. For example, I found a tutorial on YouTube that helped me refine my prompts, and then I could create better lesson plans" (No.18). Educators sharing AI-generated "alternative futures" collages that "catalyze discussions… on how AI might transform teaching and learning, its impact on the role of teachers, and broader societal implications" (No.7).

#### SC14 — Community support 🟡

- **Description.** Establishment of sustained learning communities for ongoing mutual support, resource sharing, and collective growth.
- **When to use.** For **long-term adoption** beyond a single course; for isolated practitioners without mentors (rural schools, SMEs); for in-service teachers implementing AI-based lessons who hit real-world challenges; cross-institutional and cross-border knowledge exchange.
- **In practice.** Community support "provided for lesson reflection, offering suggestions and feedback to enhance teaching effectiveness… helping teachers tackle challenges and facilitating efficient adjustments in instructional strategies during lesson enactment," delivered by senior engineers and college teachers with expertise in technology-assisted language learning (No.26). A global community for educators to share insights on both AI technology and preserving teacher–student rapport, fostering "a more inclusive and human-centred approach to technology adoption" (No.17).

### C6 — AI-supported design

**Description.** Designing *with* AI as a generative partner: co-creating new content, adapting AI output to pedagogical and cultural context, and refining it through iterative multi-turn prompting. This is where prompting skill, subject knowledge, and pedagogical reasoning converge.

**When to use.** When the learning goal is producing AI-integrated materials (lessons, content, assessments, documents); when AI outputs are generic and need contextualization; whenever learners must develop durable, tool-agnostic prompting skills.

**In practice.** Novice and experienced EFL teachers interact with ChatGPT before class to plan lessons, prepare authentic materials, and decide how to teach specific content — "the final plan was a human–AI product, not a copy of the AI's first draft" (No.2). A pre-service teacher refining a reading-comprehension activity: "After my first two prompts, I found the ChatGPT-generated reading comprehension prompts were too simplistic for the grade levels I specified. They missed the cultural and linguistic considerations. I would have to prompt the chat box with these requests" (No.25).

#### SC15 — Co-creation with AI 🟡

- **Description.** Co-creation of new instructional content with AI tools, emphasizing teacher–AI collaboration (reframing SQD2's *design activities*).
- **When to use.** When the goal is *producing* rather than consuming; planning and preparation support; when flexibility (multiple versions) is valuable; for learners ready for dialogic interaction with AI; for advanced co-creation goals (Marco).
- **In practice.** Teachers co-designed lesson plans with ChatGPT before their classes, using it to receive guidance, prepare authentic materials, and structure content (No.2). "AI is capable of providing teachers with several lesson plans at the same time… Plan A and Plan B for their teaching easily" (No.12). "AI has been quite effective in helping me develop an interactive and interpersonal lesson plan" (S15, No.15).

#### SC16 — AI-informed pedagogical adaptation 🟡

- **Description.** Adaptation and contextualization of AI-generated or existing materials to align with instructional goals, learner levels, cultural contexts, and curriculum standards (reframing SQD2's *constructive alignment*).
- **When to use.** Whenever AI output is too generic; in **culturally diverse contexts** where the model's defaults may not fit; when alignment with standards or assessment frameworks is required; for subject-specific work where domain knowledge is needed to judge quality ("My ChatGPT skills have been improved, but I believe the ELT knowledge is also so important to get refined results," No.18).
- **In practice.** A teacher adapting song lyrics to her learners' comprehension levels, and a colleague using ChatGPT to refine educational song lyrics to align with lesson objectives for EFL learners (No.27). The *Dumplings for Lily* case: ChatGPT's reading prompts "too simplistic for the grade levels… missed the cultural and linguistic considerations" until additional prompts added them (No.25). Pre-service teachers generating MATH41 practice problems, examples, or visual aids aligned to self-formulated learning objectives, then iteratively refining lesson plans (No.22).

#### SC17 — Iterative prompting 🔵

- **Description.** Iterative prompting and refinement of prompts through multi-turn dialogue to develop effective AI interaction skills. **New in iSQD.**
- **When to use.** In any design work with generative AI; with learners who habitually accept the first output; the primary vehicle for building durable, tool-agnostic prompting competence; to grow prompting literacy and self-efficacy together (confidence rises as outputs improve).
- **In practice.** Trainees asked to revise AI-generated lesson plans, formative and summative assessment tools, and rubrics multiple times: "You need to know how to use it properly… We should utilize several prompts and not settle for the first one" (No.10). "The more I refined prompts to ChatGPT outputs, the more likely I'm to create useful lesson content, which in turn made me feel more confident in my tech skills" (No.18). Moving from a general "What is your comment on this essay?" to specific follow-ups on content, expression, and developmental level: "If I continuously pose follow-up questions to ChatGPT, it will… go a step further… which will certainly enhance the quality of the feedback" (PST01, No.29).

## 6. Synthesis 4 — A cycle of authentic experiences 🟢🟡🔵

> **Strategy statement:** "Practicing and refining AI-integration, and adapting AI-content to their authentic setting, feedback is needed to support individuals to use AI in their practice."

**Description.** An iterative cycle of authentic, real-world application followed by continuous feedback — the model notes **human feedback is more valued than machine feedback** for translating experience into practice. The progression is deliberate: **low-risk practice (microteaching) → sustained authentic implementation → humanizing the AI content → systematic refinement**, with ethical inquiry embedded in the practice itself. Two strategies are new to iSQD: *emotional enrichment of AI content* (countering the mechanical, impersonal quality of AI output) and *responsible AI use* (ethical inquiry embedded in practice activities rather than taught as a separate topic — ethical AI competency develops through situated judgment).

**When to use.**

- *Context:* the practical phase of teacher education, internships, and corporate pilots; whenever the goal is *transfer to real practice* rather than classroom competence; in settings with access to peers (for microteaching) and real learners (for classroom teaching).
- *Learners:* anyone who has completed familiarization and design practice; especially valuable for learners who need proof that AI works in *their* real setting (Elena: pilot in low-stakes HR processes first) and for advanced learners doing authentic project work (Marco).

**In practice.** Teachers implemented AI-integrated lessons in their weekly EFL instruction for three months (eight 40-minute sessions), evaluated efficacy by analysing student progress and observation notes, shared insights, critiqued each other's methods, and refined their lessons (No.26, No.27).

### C7 — AI-integrated teaching cycle

**Description.** The practice arc itself: from supportive, low-risk microteaching to sustained authentic classroom (or workplace) implementation, with AI content enriched by human sensitivity.

**When to use.** After foundational familiarization and design; sequenced low-risk → authentic so that failure stays cheap and confidence compounds; whenever AI outputs reach learners and need humanizing.

**In practice.** Pre-service teachers conducted 20-minute segments of their designed lessons in microteaching sessions, teaching peers who role-played as students, using the materials they had created with MATH41 (No.22). A five-month, five-session workshop program on lesson planning with the Questions app was followed by three months of real implementation (No.26).

#### SC18 — AI-assisted microteaching 🟢

- **Description.** Microteaching activities involving AI-assisted instruction conducted in supportive, low-risk environments (e.g., workshops) to practice and refine teaching approaches.
- **When to use.** **Before** real implementation; with anxious learners and novice teachers; as rehearsal where peer "students" are forgiving and feedback is immediate; to surface explicit decision-making about *which* AI to use and *why*.
- **In practice.** In a five-month, five-session workshop on lesson planning, microteaching helped teachers integrate the Questions app into English-speaking instruction — the low-stakes setting surfaced the moment of truth: "any teacher who wants to integrate technology has to ask himself/herself several questions before integration," which forced informed judgment about which AI to use and why (No.24, No.26). Pre-service teachers teaching 20-minute lesson excerpts to peer "students" with MATH41 materials (No.22).

#### SC19 — AI-integrated classroom teaching 🟢

- **Description.** Implementation of AI-assisted instruction in authentic settings, with sustained practice with real learners (direct transfer of SQD2's *Real world*).
- **When to use.** The culminating phase — internships, practica, in-service pilots; sustained over weeks–months; when the evidence base for a program includes real transfer; to gain insight into "diverse contextual needs" that workshops can't reveal; for advanced learners' authentic project work (Marco).
- **In practice.** Teachers ran Questions-app-integrated lessons "in their weekly EFL instructions for three months (eight sessions, 40 minutes each)" (No.26). "Weeks 5–7: Implementation (Experimentation Phase) — The teachers implemented the CALL lessons they designed in their classrooms… to investigate the outcomes in an authentic setting" (No.27). "The need to provide opportunities for teachers to experiment their lesson plans in the actual classroom contexts to gain more insights for dealing with diverse contextual needs" (No.24).

#### SC20 — Emotional enrichment of AI content 🔵

- **Description.** Enhancing AI-generated content and feedback with emotional tone and human sensitivity to reduce mechanical or impersonal qualities. **New in iSQD.**
- **When to use.** Whenever AI-generated feedback or content reaches learners — especially young or vulnerable learners; in evaluative or sensitive situations (corrections); a direct response to evidence that impersonal AI feedback damages motivation and wellbeing; to protect teacher–student rapport.
- **In practice.** A case study of an intelligent tutoring system: "If you tell the kids you are wrong without telling them why, their pride is gone… The kids got frustrated… they could not handle that" (Sam, No.8). In contrast, pre-service teachers who enriched ChatGPT's feedback: "We should add some subjective emotions when we give feedback to students, and we should not make our feedback look hard and cold," embedding culturally resonant encouragement that ChatGPT alone could not generate (No.29). A teacher discovering VR reduced face-to-face interaction and choosing how to "balance technological innovation with personal engagement… ensuring that technology enhances rather than dilutes education quality" (Hua, No.17).

### C8 — AI-integrated teaching improvement

**Description.** After and during authentic use: systematic refinement of AI integration and critical, ethical evaluation of its use — the feedback-and-refinement loop that closes the cycle.

**When to use.** After each implementation round, using feedback and learner data; whenever ethical stakes (fairness, privacy, integrity, over-reliance) require an explicit checkpoint; as the continuous improvement loop that keeps AI use responsible and effective.

**In practice.** Groups critiqued AI-generated lesson plans with structured prompts: *Evaluate the pros and cons. What is missing? What are the barriers to learning? What else should we consider? Reflect on the process of using AI tools* (No.10).

#### SC21 — AI-integrated lesson refinement 🟡

- **Description.** Systematic refinement of AI integration in instruction based on evidence from the teaching cycle.
- **When to use.** After implementation, using feedback and learner data; in communities where critique is structured; to close the practice–reflection–redesign loop; when student progress data or observation notes indicate problems.
- **In practice.** The five-prompt group critique of generated lesson plans (above, No.10). Post-teaching reflection journals evaluating lesson design effectiveness, how the AI tool "contributed to or altered their approach," challenges, and what "they would improve next time" — explicitly connecting theory to practice (No.22). Teachers "analyz[ing] student progress and their observation notes… shared insights, critiqued the effectiveness of their own and each other's methods, and refined their CALL lessons" (No.27).

#### SC22 — Responsible AI use 🔵

- **Description.** Critical reflection on the appropriateness, ethical implications, and limitations of AI use in teaching — **embedded in practice activities, not as a separate lecture** (ethical AI competency develops through situated judgment). **New in iSQD.**
- **When to use.** Throughout authentic cycles; whenever AI use involves privacy, bias, accountability, or equity decisions; to counter over-reliance; for sceptics whose concerns must be answered inside real work (Elena: fairness, accountability, employee trust).
- **In practice.** Teachers valuing AI's ability to offer interactive feedback tailored to individual proficiency "while cautioning against an overdependence on AI… a balanced and ethical integration of AI in educational settings" (No.27). Professional development that includes "how they and their students can approach their use of AI tools critically, being aware of potential bias and inaccurate information… tools for generating lesson plans may be timesavers, [but] they must also be used judiciously" (No.9). Educators engaged in "collective inquiry… for various ethical concerns… potential dilemmas in AI integration, privacy issues, transparency, accountability in AI decision-making processes, and the risk of perpetuating inequalities or silencing minoritized voices" (No.7). For Elena: piloting AI in low-stakes HR processes "followed by human feedback and ethical evaluation… safeguarding fairness, transparency, and employee trust."

## 7. Synthesis 5 — Affective dimension 🟡

> **Strategy statement:** "Emotional reactions, attitudes and beliefs, and self-efficacy should be attended to when preparing individuals for developing their AI competencies."

**Description.** A core, cross-cutting condition of all other strategies: AI integration "often triggers strong emotional responses; positive experiences boost confidence, while negative ones can lead to frustration that hinders adoption." Learners' attitudes, perceptions, **trust**, and value beliefs toward AI — plus their confidence and self-assessment of their AI competence (self-efficacy) — determine whether they adopt or resist. SQD2's three sub-categories (attitudes and beliefs, emotional sphere, sensitivity to tech gaps) are **reconfigured** in iSQD as emotional reactions, attitudes & beliefs, and **self-efficacy**; trust in AI-generated outputs emerged as a prominent concern. The dimension also carries a "Sensitivity to Tech Gaps" — acknowledging that disparities in digital competence and access can marginalize some learners.

**When to use.**

- *Context:* **continuously, across every phase** — not a module to "cover," but a design condition: check in on emotions at onboarding, after frustrating failures, and before high-stakes authentic use; build affective checkpoints into program design.
- *Learners:* especially **sceptical, anxious, or low-self-efficacy professionals** (Elena's "emotional sphere" must be actively mitigated); also high-attitude learners, whose affective work shifts to *digital identity* — interpreting their beliefs and values as they balance human skills with AI automation (Marco).

**In practice.** A program that "fosters an environment that prioritizes exploration over enforcement… can help alleviate the anxiety associated with AI adoption and empower educators to make informed, confident decisions about when and how to incorporate AI into their classrooms" (No.7). A novice teacher describing the tightrope honestly: *"On one hand, I'm a big believer in the power of personal connections in education… On the other hand, there's a nagging worry that we might start relying too much on tech, maybe even diluting those personal touches. The training forced me to confront this tension"* (No.17).

### C9 — Affective dimension

**Description.** The set of affective strategies: attending to emotions during AI use, shaping attitudes and beliefs (and correcting misconceptions), and building self-efficacy through successful experience.

**When to use.** In program design (build affective checkpoints in), when resistance or anxiety surfaces, and when trust in AI output needs to be established.

#### SC23 — Emotional reactions 🟡

- **Description.** Emotions experienced by individuals during AI use — anxiety, worry, enjoyment, satisfaction, and other emotions.
- **When to use.** At onboarding (novelty + pressure), after frustrating failures, and whenever design choices (deadlines, enforcement) could poison the emotional climate; to convert negative affect into support (feedback, scaffolding) rather than letting it block adoption.
- **In practice.** Educators reported "significant pressure to integrate AI into their teaching practices, leading to confusion and uncertainty," often because AI integration was experienced as a *mandatory requirement*; the program that prioritized "exploration over enforcement" measurably reduced adoption anxiety (No.7). A teacher's "tightrope" tension: "a nagging worry that we might start relying too much on tech, maybe even diluting those personal touches and individual teaching styles we cherish" (No.17). Reflection with ChatGPT leaving novice teachers "more motivated, creative, and satisfied… and emotionally felt better" (No.2).

#### SC24 — Attitudes & beliefs 🟡

- **Description.** Attitudes, perceptions, **trust**, and value beliefs toward AI tools; including correcting misconceptions about AI's capabilities.
- **When to use.** With sceptical cohorts; when resistance is *belief-based* ("AI will replace teachers," "AI can't be trusted"); whenever trust in AI output must be calibrated — not blind trust, but informed confidence; programs must actively "create conditions that are conducive to generating trust."
- **In practice.** A trainee's shift: "The main takeaway… is that AI is a brave new world but can actually work as something we should embrace rather than shy away from" (No.10). PST07: "Before the training, I viewed ChatGPT as a tool limited to basic tasks like checking punctuation… after the training, I realized that with appropriate prompts, it can generate reliable and useful suggestions" (No.29). A documented knowledge gap: "some pre-service teachers believed that AI could replace traditional teaching methods entirely, leading to resistance in integrating these tools into their pedagogical practices" (No.12).

#### SC25 — Self-efficacy 🟡

- **Description.** Confidence and self-assessment of one's competencies to use AI tools in teaching (or professional practice).
- **When to use.** As a growth target in every phase; for **low-confidence learners** (Elena — build confidence without increasing anxiety) — the strongest predictor of whether they will keep using AI after training; build it through early wins, modelling, and visible skill growth; to exploit the mutually reinforcing cycle: better prompting skill → better outputs → more confidence → more engagement.
- **In practice.** "As I worked more with ChatGPT, I found that my skills get better and better over time. It's like the more I refined prompts to ChatGPT outputs, the more likely I'm to create useful lesson content, which in turn made me feel more confident in my tech skills" (No.18). The documented "mutually reinforcing positive cycle": higher self-efficacy → more effective AI-assisted feedback → higher self-efficacy (No.29). Bite-sized microlearning: "Breaking down GenAI learning into small chunks makes it less daunting" — 14 of 19 teachers reporting it helped them overcome AI anxiety (No.14).

## 8. Synthesis 6 — Institutional vision 🟢🟡

> **Strategy statement (derived):** "AI competence development requires institutional conditions — curriculum, frameworks, training and incentives, guidelines, and leadership support — so that support is systemic rather than left to individual effort."

**Description.** The **meso level**: the conditions under which S1–S5 can be implemented systematically and sustained. SQD2's two institutional categories (pedagogical vision; group commitment) are **expanded into three** in iSQD — AI-integrated curriculum, AI-focused training & incentives, and institutional support — because AI's novelty creates *normative uncertainty* that institutions must resolve (guidelines) and subject-matter variation that requires discipline-specific design. The evidence is blunt: without institutional and leadership support, "the burden of AI integration falls on the individual educator… leading to inconsistent adoption and missed opportunities."

**When to use.**

- *Context:* when designing a **program, department, school district policy, or corporate L&D offering** — before or alongside S1–S5, because adoption is documented to be hindered when these conditions are missing; when adoption is fragmented or blocked by missing policies.
- *Learners/stakeholders:* program designers, department heads, HR/L&D leaders, institutional leadership. Dr. Clara explicitly "requires a clear Pedagogical Vision from her institution that provides ethical frameworks and structured professional learning."

**In practice.** A study found GenAI adoption "somewhat hindered by a lack of institutional and administrative guidelines": a university policy that simply told each faculty member to set their own AI policy "places the responsibility on faculty members, who may lack the essential knowledge… This in turn creates uncertainty and becomes a barrier to innovation" (No.23).

### C10 — AI-integrated curriculum

**Description.** The structural backbone: systematic course design for AI competence — structured and progressive AI courses, grounded in established educational frameworks, and differentiated by discipline.

**When to use.** When AI competence must be a *learning outcome* of the curriculum (not an add-on event); when designing or renewing courses, degrees, or corporate learning paths; when fidelity, replicability, or alignment with recognized standards (e.g., UNESCO's AI competency framework for teachers) is required.

**In practice.** A scripted GenAI workshop was "intentionally structured to guide participants through progressive learning phases that lead to meaningful critical insights… 'eureka moments'… unexpected scenarios that challenge their assumptions, create cognitive dissonance, and trigger deeper reflection" (No.4).

#### SC26 — AI courses 🟡

- **Description.** AI courses with clearly defined weekly or modular arrangements and a progressive learning pathway.
- **When to use.** Course design and renewal; when learners need a coherent arc from foundations to responsible integration; for replicable, shareable course formats.
- **In practice.** The scripted, progressive workshop with "eureka moments" and immersive "walk in the agent's shoes" experiences (No.4). A three-session "AI clinic" with explicit learning outcomes: (LO1) explain basic LLM architecture and limitations; (LO2) outline three ethical principles for classroom use; (LO3) compose prompts with role–task–context framing; (LO4) iteratively refine prompts for relevance and bias mitigation; (LO5) evaluate AI outputs against curricular standards; (LO6) draft a lesson snippet embedding AI-generated materials responsibly — with a syllabus and rubrics provided for replication (No.6).

#### SC27 — Educational frameworks 🟢

- **Description.** Systematic design of AI teaching courses based on established frameworks (e.g., IBL, TPACK, UNESCO's AI competency framework for teachers).
- **When to use.** When design **fidelity and alignment** with recognized standards matter (accreditation, institutional consistency); when teams need a shared design language; for university course design and PD programs requiring legitimacy; for lecturers who think in frameworks (Dr. Clara).
- **In practice.** An AI-focused course "designed following UNESCO's AI competency framework for teachers" (No.15). An IBL-based intervention in which pre-service teachers "designed lesson plans, conducted microteaching, and demonstrated their AI-powered lessons for group observations and reflections" (No.27). A workshop "grounded in Harris et al.'s (2009) TPACK framework," with a detailed plan collaboratively developed by two doctoral students and two professors for fidelity (No.29). Dr. Clara: using TPACK "to align AI tools with her specific content knowledge and pedagogical goals… ensuring AI supports learning rather than replacing them."

#### SC28 — Discipline-specific AI integration 🟡

- **Description.** Design of differentiated AI training programs according to subject characteristics (an adapted response to AI's variation across domains).
- **When to use.** In subject departments and discipline-based PD; when AI affordances differ sharply by field (language vs. math vs. humanities vs. HR); for advanced PD stages after general AI literacy; to avoid one generic training for all.
- **In practice.** A staged PD concept: "the initial teacher PD could focus on AI knowledge and pedagogy enhancement whereas the advanced PD concerns integrating AI education into different subject areas" (No.8). "Prompt engineering training should be part of teacher-education programs, e.g., as a last or additional part after each seminar or course, and it should be trained specifically with regard to subject-matter requirements" (No.16). Student teachers forming "groups based on their specialization to develop lesson plans," presented with instant feedback from faculty and students (No.24).

### C11 — AI-focused training & incentives

**Description.** Sustaining competence beyond the course: ongoing professional learning that keeps pace with rapid AI development, plus institutional incentives that motivate participation and application.

**When to use.** After initial training (to prevent skill decay in a fast-moving field); in organizations where adoption depends on policy levers (pay, certification, career progression); where participation is voluntary and needs motivating; to recognize and reward AI competence.

**In practice.** A teacher educator: "Things have been changing very quickly. I have to update myself because this is new to me. And when I know more about this, I can help my student teachers use these tools more effectively pedagogically" (No.20). Institutions offering "paid GenAI learning sessions, subscriptions, micro-credentials or some certification that can be added to the teacher's CV" (No.23).

#### SC29 — AI-related professional learning 🟢

- **Description.** Provision of ongoing professional learning opportunities to enable teachers to keep pace with the rapid development of AI (direct transfer of SQD2's *professional learning*).
- **When to use.** In any sustained program, indefinitely; in faculty/student learning communities where students actively participate in learning about GenAI; whenever the tool landscape shifts (new model releases, new policies).
- **In practice.** "All of the participants indicated they need professional development and support to increase their GenAI competence" (No.20). Faculty and student learning communities in courses, supported by frameworks that "prioritize thinking and instructor support to learn about AI" (No.23).

#### SC30 — Incentives 🟡

- **Description.** Institutional incentive policies (e.g., certification, rewards, or career development opportunities) encouraging teachers to participate in AI training and instructional applications.
- **When to use.** When designing organizational adoption strategy; where voluntary uptake is slow; to align individual careers with institutional AI goals.
- **In practice.** "They can offer incentives such as paid GenAI learning sessions, subscriptions, micro-credentials or some certification that can be added to the teacher's CV. Administrators can support collaboration among institutions and educators to establish clear guidelines on the usage of AI and academic integrity" (No.23).

### C12 — Institutional support

**Description.** The governance and leadership layer: institutional policies and ethical frameworks for AI use, and strategic leadership commitment with resources.

**When to use.** When adoption is stalled by ambiguity (no guidelines), by missing resources, or by absent accountability; whenever academic-integrity or fairness concerns need a formal answer; to give learners a safe space for experimentation.

**In practice.** Pre-service teachers "expressed reluctance to rely on such tools for writing assignments due to concerns about potential reprimands. They desired clearer guidelines on how GenAI tools like ChatGPT could be appropriately integrated into their academic work" (No.25).

#### SC31 — Institutional AI guidelines 🟡

- **Description.** Development of institutional policies and ethical frameworks for AI use, accompanied by normative guidance established through cross-departmental collaboration.
- **When to use.** **When the absence of guidelines is itself a barrier** (documented in multiple studies — absence directly hinders adoption and creates fear); when academic integrity, privacy, or fairness questions must be answered consistently; before scaling any AI initiative.
- **In practice.** A documented barrier: university policy "promotes autonomy by emphasizing that each class should implement its own AI policy… but places the responsibility on faculty… creating uncertainty and becoming a barrier to innovation" (No.23). The remedy in one study: "researchers and school teachers could collaborate to develop a guideline for using ChatGPT in writing feedback… including prompts tailored to writing contexts and scoring criteria, along with common issues encountered in ChatGPT interactions and corresponding solutions" (No.29). Dr. Clara: requiring "a clear Pedagogical Vision from her institution that provides ethical frameworks and structured professional learning."

#### SC32 — Leadership support 🟡

- **Description.** Strategic support from institutional leadership, including resource provision and decision-making commitment, to promote the systematic implementation of AI integration.
- **When to use.** At the start of any organizational AI strategy; when resources, time, and authority must be guaranteed; when adoption is inconsistent across units; to drive change beyond individual champions and fund resources and professional learning.
- **In practice.** "Educational leaders reported higher levels of readiness, reflecting their strategic roles… Leaders were more likely to identify their institutions as being in advanced stages of AI planning or implementation, underscoring their role in driving organizational change" (No.13). The counter-evidence: "There is a need to address institutional shortcomings… establishing clear guidelines, providing dedicated resources, and implementing professional learning opportunities… Without institutional and leadership support, the burden of AI integration falls on the individual educator, which can potentially lead to inconsistent adoption and missed opportunities" (No.23).

### How it plays out

**Elena.** Her goal: foundational AI literacy to integrate AI responsibly in HR practices, reduce anxiety (hers and her colleagues'), and make informed decisions about where AI supports human judgment. In the Pedagogical Constitution these goals carry competency-framework codes: acquire basic AI literacy (D01, D04); understanding how AI can support administrative efficiency without replacing human roles (D10); reducing AI-related anxiety (R17). Because she has a lower ICT attitude, the research indicates she will rely more heavily on feedback for confirmation and support as she develops integrated designs. Familiarization is therefore HR-specific and guided (AI-assisted screening or reporting) to prevent cognitive overload, with role models demonstrating tangible benefits; reflection is structured (short decision rationales, evaluation checklists) to make explicit where AI supports decision-making and where human responsibility must remain central; authentic experience starts in **low-stakes HR processes** followed by human feedback and ethical evaluation; and the affective work covers both her emotional sphere and sensitivity to tech gaps, so adoption doesn't marginalize lower-competence employees.

**Marco.** His goal: advanced AI competencies — effective co-creation with AI, critical evaluation of AI-generated outputs, and control over AI-supported work processes. As a high-attitude learner he thrives through collaborative design: peer tasks to articulate design choices, discuss prompting strategies, and refine AI-supported solutions. His reflective work focuses on evaluating outputs for accuracy, limitations, and appropriateness, supported by AI-mediated reflection on his design and problem-solving choices. Authentic project work with iterative peer/mentor feedback refines AI integration in realistic contexts, and his affective work is digital-identity work: interpreting his beliefs and values as he balances human skills with AI automation.

**Dr. Clara.** Her goal: AI competencies for responsible course and assessment design, integrating AI thoughtfully while promoting critical AI literacy and maintaining disciplinary standards. She needs structured opportunities to reflect on the role and limitations of AI in teaching and assessment — documented reflection and group discussion support her in evaluating AI-generated materials and negotiating academic-integrity concerns. Collaborative design uses frameworks like TPACK to align AI tools with her content knowledge and pedagogical goals, with colleagues supporting co-creation, adaptation, and sharing. She then runs an iterative cycle — experimenting with AI-integrated teaching (e.g., a flipped classroom) and receiving feedback from a collegial network of mentors and peers on redesigned assignments and authentic AI use. At the meso level she requires a clear pedagogical vision: ethical frameworks and structured professional learning from her institution.

## 10. The AIM-PRO Pedagogical Constitution

*(Source: AIM-PRO monthly meeting presentation, 2 April 2026 — "AIM-PRO Pedagogical Constitution," WP5, tasks T5.1 "Pedagogical requirements" (M8–M16) and T5.2 "Pedagogical framework adaptation and validation" (M17–M24).)*

**What it is.** The Pedagogical Constitution is AIM-PRO's set of pedagogical principles that **guide the creation of its tools, resources, and learning scenarios**. It operationalizes iSQD at the project level: to ensure that AIM-PRO's educational interventions are "beneficial and deeply effective," the context of the user and their AI competency goals should be matched with the effective strategies for developing digital competencies identified in the evidence base (Tondeur et al., 2025).

**Why it is needed.** AIM-PRO utilizes Open Educational Resources (OERs), Generative AI, Model-Driven Engineering (MDE), and Digital Twin technologies to create personalized, sustainable, and inclusive learning environments. Building AI competencies requires more than technical know-how: it demands a mix of practical skills, critical thinking, ethical awareness, and the ability to adapt to fast-changing technologies (Howard et al., 2025).

**Vision statement.** *"AIM-PRO leverages AI to develop adaptive curricula that tailor learning scenarios and content to individual preferences, existing knowledge, and learning objectives"* — concretely: adapting to the needs of the learner; based on interaction with the trainer; and the trainer as the one who will facilitate AI literacy education in their context.

**Core pedagogical principles.** The constitution restates the five iSQD syntheses as principles that all AIM-PRO interventions must respect (S6 — the institutional conditions — is what makes them possible):

1. **Modelling and exploration** are essential to familiarise individuals with AI technologies. *(→ S1)*
2. Learners must **critically reflect on and engage with** both the potential and the limitations of AI technologies. *(→ S2)*
3. A **range of collaborative design activities** is necessary to train individuals in creating environments to work with AI. *(→ S3)*
4. A **cycle of authentic experiences and feedback** is needed to support individuals to use AI in their practice. *(→ S4)*
5. **Affective characteristics** should be taken into account when preparing individuals for developing their AI competencies. *(→ S5)*

**Structure of the constitution.**

| Layer | Requirements |
| --- | --- |
| **Pedagogical** (T5.1 / D5.1) | Learner-centered · Outcome-oriented · Based on AI-specific strategies |
| **Instructional design** | Concise and immediately applicable · Responsive to diverse learners' needs (formal, non-formal, and informal) · Integrates cutting-edge technologies |
| **MIND** | *MIND — microlearning AI-integrated instructional design*, the instructional-design model underlying the scenarios (The MIND model, [doi:10.1038/s41598-025-24910-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-24910-y)) |
| **Examples based on personas** | Every learning scenario maps *persona → competency goal → strategy* (see §9) |
| **Technological ecosystem** | The constitution also targets the surrounding ecosystem — OERs, Generative AI, Model-Driven Engineering, Digital Twins |

**Worked example (Elena).** The constitution instantiates the framework for a specific learner: sector SME; competency goals with framework codes — acquire basic AI literacy (D01, D04), understanding how AI can support administrative efficiency without replacing human roles (D10), reducing AI-related anxiety (R17); and matched strategies — *Modelling* (observe role models like other successful SME managers demonstrating tangible AI benefits), *Continuous feedback* (lower ICT attitude → she relies more heavily on feedback for confirmation and support as she develops integrated learning designs), and *Addressing the affective dimension* (focus on her "Emotional Sphere" to mitigate stress and frustration; address "Sensitivity to Tech Gaps" so AI adoption doesn't marginalize employees with lower digital competence).
